Tier 1: taking the round trips out of the worker

Batching moved the bottleneck off the write path but left its own tail behind — a Redis round trip per error event, a triple round trip per breadcrumb, and two full copies of every job made to protect a branch that already had them. Four edits, 228 lines. At saturation the worker writes 18.5% more per second on 18.6% less CPU; at half load, over sixty seconds, it does nothing at all. Then the runs were extended to ten minutes, and one of them silently lost 47% of the events the edge had already acknowledged. That finding is not about Tier 1, and it outranks it.

One run silently lost 47% of the events it had accepted

In the first baseline soak the worker fell behind at about the five-minute mark, RSS jumped from 45 MB to 461 MB, one request stalled for 3.4 seconds, and write throughput dropped to roughly a tenth. The backlog then passed the stream's MAXLEN of one million and Redis began trimming unread entries. 53.3% of accepted events reached Postgres. The ingest logged not one warning, and crebain reported 100% accepted, 0 errors throughout — because from the edge's point of view that was entirely true.

This is not a Tier 1 result and must not be read as one. It happened once in three baseline runs; the third baseline run was clean (50 MB, 100% delivered) and indistinguishable from Tier 1. With three runs against two, and the bad runs clustered at the start of the sequence, the build is only one of several candidate explanations — accumulated machine state across back-to-back ten-minute runs is another, and I did not control for it. Tier 1 has not been shown to fix this, and has not been shown to be free of it.

What the soak does establish, independent of which build is at fault, is that the ingest has an intermittent degradation mode that ends in silent data loss, and that nothing in the system reports it. The MAXLEN trim is the mechanism that converts “the worker is behind” into “the events are gone”, and it is unconditional and unalarmed. A backlog metric would be worth more than the next round of round-trip removal.

The narrower lesson is about method, and it indicts all three of these reports: every benchmark in this series before now was sixty seconds long — and sixty seconds is shorter than the time this failure takes to appear.

06Correctness

Grouping PFADD is the only edit that could be silently wrong. If the member slice were sent as one argument instead of many, the call would still succeed, still reply 1, and still leave a working HyperLogLog — it would just be counting a concatenation as one person, and every downstream check would stay green.

08What this did not touch

  • The workflow fold. acc.workflows is still a plain Vec replayed one autocommit at a time, so an app that stamps signals with a workflow id gets one sequential Postgres round trip per signal — against frequently the same row. It is gated on the id being present, so it costs nothing today, and crebain generates no workflows, which is why no benchmark here or in the previous two reports can see it. It is a cliff, not a slope.
  • The accept path. Still 3 + N sequential Redis round trips per request, no in-process DSN cache, two SHA-256 hashes of the same key, a full header-map clone. Untouched deliberately: the previous report's claim that the edge saturates at 20k rps has not been re-checked over UDS, and the 1M-concurrency work already established that an apparent accept-rate collapse can be the generator's ephemeral-port wall rather than the server.
  • INGEST_BATCH_SIZE. Still the inherited 50, still never tuned. It now matters more than it did: every saving in this report scales with how full a batch is.
  • The mechanism behind section 05. Not established, and not attributed. It appeared once in three baseline runs and zero times in two Tier 1 runs, which is enough to take seriously and nowhere near enough to blame a build. Characterising it needs runs long enough and repeated enough to get a rate, in randomised build order, on a machine returned to a known state between runs — plus a heap profile while it is happening.
  • The MAXLEN policy. The stream trims at one million entries with nothing watching. That is what turns a slow worker into lost data, on any build. A backlog metric derived from stream offsets — not from XLEN, which counts acked entries and reads near the cap regardless — and an alert on it, is the highest-value item on this list.
